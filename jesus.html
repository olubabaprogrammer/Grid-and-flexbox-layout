The National Youth Service Corps (NYSC) is a one-year compulsory service for all Nigerian university and polytechnic graduates. Created in 1973 after the Civil War, its goal is to promote national unity by placing graduates in states other than their home regions. Corps members serve in education, health, agriculture, and government — and often help run elections, which many see as a sign of their neutrality and reliability. In 2024, their monthly allowance was raised to ₦77,000. Over 5.5 million Nigerians have served since the program began — more than the size of Nigeria’s army. It’s not just about service — it’s about building a shared national identity.

Fuel Prices in Nigeria – March 2026 Petrol is now ₦1,027 per liter on average across Nigeria — up sharply since subsidies ended. Diesel costs around ₦1,758 per liter. Prices vary by state and are still rising due to global oil market pressure, especially after the Iran-Israel-U.S. war began in February 2026. The new Dangote Refinery sells petrol at ₦1,200 per liter at its pumps — higher than the national average. Many hoped local refining would lower prices, but costs are still tied to global oil and production expenses. Since 2023, fuel prices have more than doubled. This has pushed transport fares up, hurt households, and worsened inflation. Many Nigerians now struggle to afford daily travel or run generators.

Nigerians are reacting with anger, frustration, and resignation to the soaring fuel prices. Many blame the government for removing subsidies without providing alternatives or cushioning the impact. Protests have erupted in major cities like Lagos and Abuja, with citizens demanding price controls or subsidies be restored — though the government insists it can’t afford them . Transport fares have jumped sharply, forcing many to walk or skip work. Small businesses are shutting down or cutting staff as fuel costs eat into profits. “We’re not just paying for fuel — we’re paying for survival,” one Lagos taxi driver told a local reporter . Some are turning to alternatives — like solar power, bicycles, or carpooling — but these are out of reach for most. Social media is flooded with memes and rants, with hashtags like #FuelPriceRage and #WeAreStarving trending daily. Despite the pain, many feel powerless. “They took the subsidy, but didn’t give us jobs or roads or electricity,” said one university graduate. “Now we pay more for less — and no one listens.”

The state of the church in Nigeria is marked by explosive growth but also deep spiritual challenges, particularly around materialism, declining sanctity, and a lack of genuine spiritual maturity. Nigeria has the highest number of churches in Africa, with over 10,000 new religious movements registered by 1980 and a continued surge in the 21st century, largely driven by Pentecostal and charismatic movements 1. This growth has been fueled by a desire for hope and prosperity, especially among the youth and those facing economic hardship 2. However, this expansion has often prioritized numerical increase over spiritual depth, leading to a phenomenon described as “a mile wide and an inch deep” Christianity

A significant concern is the commercialization of faith. Many churches emphasize prosperity theology, where financial giving is framed as an investment in one’s material and spiritual future 3. This has led to a culture where material wealth is often equated with divine favor, undermining traditional Christian values of humility and service 1. The focus on wealth, miracles, and visible success has created a “prosperity gospel” that attracts followers but may not foster deep spiritual commitment

This shift has contributed to declining spiritual standards. Despite the massive growth in church numbers, there is a noted decline in actual spiritual commitment, with many believers showing little interest in moral or ethical development 1. The proliferation of churches has also led to fraud, criminality, and a lack of accountability, with some pastors accused of embezzlement and exploiting their congregations 1. The result is a church landscape that is numerically strong but spiritually fragile. Furthermore, inter-denominational rivalry and doctrinal fragmentation have weakened the church’s collective witness. Denominationalism often leads to conflict and disunity, which undermines the church’s ability to act as a moral and social force 6. While the church plays a vital role in education, healthcare, and social welfare 5, its influence is often compromised by internal corruption and a focus on wealth over mission. In summary, Nigeria’s church is growing rapidly, but the quality of spiritual life is in question. The church faces a critical challenge: to move beyond growth in numbers and materialism toward authentic spiritual maturity, moral integrity, and genuine consecration to the core teachings of Christianity.

In the context of Nigeria's rapidly growing but spiritually challenged church, the life of a typical Christian is marked by deep faith, daily struggle, and a tension between spiritual ideals and material realities. Many Nigerians turn to Christianity as a source of hope, especially in the face of poverty, insecurity, and corruption. Church attendance is high — often multiple times a week — and many believers are deeply committed to prayer, Bible study, and community worship. For many, the church is not just a place of worship but a source of social support, healing, and identity. However, this faith is often tested by economic hardship. Despite the prosperity gospel preached in many churches, most believers live on low incomes, and fuel prices, inflation, and unemployment make daily survival difficult. A typical Christian may work long hours, send children to church-run schools, and give tithes — often 10% of their income — even when it means going without food or medicine. Spiritual life is often practical and survival-focused. Believers may pray for healing, job opportunities, or protection from violence, especially in the north where attacks on churches and Christian communities are common. They may also rely on faith-based healing, prayer groups, and charismatic leaders for emotional and physical relief. Yet, many feel spiritually conflicted. While they attend church, give tithes, and participate in worship, they may struggle with moral compromises — such as accepting bribes, engaging in gossip, or tolerating corruption — to survive. They see their faith as a source of strength in a difficult world. For them, Christianity is not just about belief — it’s about enduring, serving, and believing that God will provide, even when life is hard.
